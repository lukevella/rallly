Scheduling a thesis or dissertation defense means finding one time that suits a whole committee, and its members rarely share a calendar. Often one of them is an external examiner in another country. Whether you're the candidate or the coordinator arranging it for them, the job is the same.

To see what separates the polls that find a date from the ones that don't, we counted the outcomes of 1,227 polls created on Rallly for a defense, a viva or an oral exam. We worked from totals only. Nobody read individual polls, and no names, email addresses or comments were used. The details are in the note at the end.

## Key findings

- **Group size matters most.** Polls with three or four people found a date for everyone 87% of the time. With seven or more it was 46%.
- **More options didn't help.** Polls with 4 to 6 options found a date as often as polls with 25 or more.
- **Most people start early.** Three in four polls were sent at least four weeks before the first date offered.
- **Near misses are common.** When no date worked for everyone, it was usually one person short.
- **Answers come quickly.** 86% of responses arrived within a week.

## Before you send anything

Check your program's regulations for four things. They decide who you ask and which dates you can offer.

- **Who sets the date.** Some universities only allow it once examiners are formally appointed, or have a central office that assigns it.
- **Who must attend.** Find out who is required, and whether a quorum or a substitute is allowed.
- **Whether anyone can attend remotely.** This often needs approval in advance.
- **How much notice is required.** Look for reading time, forms and any public announcement, then work back from your graduation deadline.

## How far in advance to start

Start at least four weeks before the earliest date you could hold the defense, and earlier for a larger committee. Half of the polls we looked at were sent about seven weeks before the first date they offered.

Starting early made the most difference for committees of five or six. Their polls found a date 75% of the time when the first option was at least four weeks away, compared with 62% at two to four weeks. Only 60 polls were in that second group, so treat the size of the gap as approximate.

## Who to include in the poll

Ask only the people who must be there, usually the candidate, the supervisor and the examiners or committee. Leave out the audience and anyone whose attendance is optional, and tell them the date once it's set.

You usually can't change the size of a committee, but you can plan for it. If seven or more people are required, start earlier, and settle before you send the poll whether one of them could attend remotely.

## How many dates to offer your committee

Offer 5 to 10 options. Polls with 4 to 6 options found a date about as often as polls with 25 or more, so a long list doesn't appear to help. The upper limit is our judgment, since a shorter poll is quicker for a busy examiner to answer.

<BarChart title="More options didn't find more dates" description="Polls with a date that worked for everyone who responded, counting if need be">
  <Bar label="4 to 6 options" sublabel="122 polls" value="77" />

Spread your options across different days rather than listing every hour of a few days. The typical poll covered eight different days. Only offer times when you can get a room, and make each option long enough for the whole event, including deliberation.

## How to set up the poll

Any scheduling poll will do. These steps use [Rallly](/scheduling-for/thesis-defense), which is free and doesn't ask your committee to create accounts.

1. Sign in to Rallly and go to [Create a poll](https://app.rallly.co/new). A free account is enough, and you'll need one to finalize the date.
2. Add a title such as "PhD defense, Biology, April". Anyone with the link can see the title and description, so leave out embargoed thesis titles.
3. Click **Add location**, or click **Add video call** to have Rallly create a Google Meet, Zoom, Microsoft Teams or Webex link when you finalize. You'll need to connect that service under **Settings** first.
4. Click **Add description** and say how long the event lasts and the date to respond by. Rallly doesn't enforce this date, so it's up to you to follow up.
5. Set the **Duration** and select your options in the calendar.
6. Leave **Require email** on, so that everyone can receive the calendar invite, and keep **Vote options** on yes, if need be and no.
7. Click **Create poll**.

### If you have an external examiner

An external examiner is often the hardest person to schedule. They don't appear in your university's calendar, and they may be several time zones away.

Rallly shows each person the options in their own time zone, so nobody has to convert anything. Include a few options that fall inside the external examiner's working day, and be careful around the weeks when clocks change, since countries change on different dates. They can respond from the link without an account.

## Share it and follow up

Click **Share**, copy the invite link and send it to your committee. Ask people to choose "if need be" for any time they could make by moving something. Polls with a date for everyone rose from 65% to 77% once these answers were counted. People can also leave a note that only you can see, which suits things like access needs.

As answers come in, the poll shows who can make each option.

## Finalize the date

Click **Finalize** and Rallly lists your options with the best supported first. Choose the date and select who to notify. Everyone you select gets an email with a calendar invite, including the video call link if you added one. Book the room the same day.

## What to do if no date works for everyone

Don't start again. Most polls without a date for everyone were only one person short, so look at the options closest to working and ask that person whether they can move something.

<BarChart title="Most polls with no date were one person short" description="Of polls with no date for everyone, those where one more person would have been enough">

If that doesn't work, check whether your regulations allow them to attend remotely or allow a substitute. As a last resort, edit the poll to add options in a period you haven't tried yet, then ask everyone to come back and answer them. Responses to your existing options are kept.

## An example

Here is how this might look for an imaginary committee of five, with a supervisor, two internal examiners, a chair and an external examiner eight hours ahead.

The coordinator checks the regulations in early April. Everyone must attend, the external examiner may join by video, and the defense has to happen before mid June. On April 20 she creates a poll with eight two hour options spread over two weeks in late May, with half of them in the morning so that they fall inside the external examiner's day. She asks for answers by April 27.

Four people respond within three days, and she emails the fifth. No option gets five yes answers, but one has four and an "if need be" from an internal examiner. She confirms with that examiner, finalizes the date and books the room.

*How we measured this: an automated query matched poll titles against a list of keywords and returned only counts and percentages. No titles, names, email addresses, comments or notes were read or exported, and nothing here can be traced back to a poll or a person. The sample is polls in our database from the last 12 months, in November 2026, with at least three responses and a title identifying a defense, viva or oral exam. Each poll was at least 14 days old, or 30 days for response timing. Free polls are deleted after their dates pass, so recent polls make up most of the sample. We can only count people who responded, and we can't see whether the group met on that date. These figures show patterns and don't prove that one thing caused another.*
