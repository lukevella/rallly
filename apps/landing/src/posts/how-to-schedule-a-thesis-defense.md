Scheduling a thesis or dissertation defense means finding one time that suits a whole committee, and its members rarely share a calendar. Often one of them is an external examiner in another country. Whether you're the candidate or the coordinator arranging it for them, the job is the same.

This guide walks through it step by step, from checking your regulations to confirming the date.

## Before you send anything

Check your program's regulations for four things. They decide who you ask and which dates you can offer.

- **Who sets the date.** Some universities only allow it once examiners are formally appointed, or have a central office that assigns it.
- **Who must attend.** Find out who is required, and whether a quorum or a substitute is allowed.
- **Whether anyone can attend remotely.** This often needs approval in advance.
- **How much notice is required.** Look for reading time, forms and any public announcement, then work back from your graduation deadline.

## How far in advance to start

Start at least four weeks before the earliest date you could hold the defense. Examiners' diaries fill up, and your program's notice period comes on top of the time it takes to agree a date. Start earlier if your committee is large or includes someone who travels a lot.

## Who to include in the poll

Ask only the people who must be there, usually the candidate, the supervisor and the examiners or committee. Leave out the audience and anyone whose attendance is optional, and tell them the date once it's set.

Every extra person makes a shared date harder to find. You usually can't change the size of a committee, but you can plan for it. If yours is large, start earlier, and settle before you send the poll whether one of them could attend remotely.

## How many dates to offer your committee

Offer 5 to 10 options. A short list is quicker for a busy examiner to answer, and you can add more later if none of them work.

Spread your options across different days rather than listing every hour of a few days. Only offer times when you can get a room, and make each option long enough for the whole event, including deliberation.

### If you have an external examiner

An external examiner is often the hardest person to schedule. They don't appear in your university's calendar, and they may be several time zones away.

Rallly shows each person the options in their own time zone, so nobody has to convert anything. Include a few options that fall inside the external examiner's working day, and be careful around the weeks when clocks change, since countries change on different dates. They can respond from the link without an account.

## Share it and follow up

Click **Share**, copy the invite link and send it to your committee. Ask people to choose "if need be" for any time they could make by moving something, since those answers often turn a near miss into a date. People can also leave a note that only you can see, which suits things like access needs.

As answers come in, the poll shows who can make each option.

Give people a week to respond, and contact anyone who hasn't answered after three days.

## Finalize the date

Click **Finalize** and Rallly lists your options with the best supported first. Choose the date and select who to notify. Everyone you select gets an email with a calendar invite, including the video call link if you added one. Book the room the same day.

## What to do if no date works for everyone

Don't start again. Look at the options that came closest. If one of them is a single person short, ask that person whether they can move something.

If that doesn't work, check whether your regulations allow them to attend remotely or allow a substitute. As a last resort, edit the poll to add options in a period you haven't tried yet, then ask everyone to come back and answer them. Responses to your existing options are kept.

## An example

Here is how this might look for an imaginary committee of five, with a supervisor, two internal examiners, a chair and an external examiner eight hours ahead.

The coordinator checks the regulations in early April. Everyone must attend, the external examiner may join by video, and the defense has to happen before mid June. On April 20 she creates a poll with eight two hour options spread over two weeks in late May, with half of them in the morning so that they fall inside the external examiner's day. She asks for answers by April 27.

Four people respond within three days, and she emails the fifth. No option gets five yes answers, but one has four and an "if need be" from an internal examiner. She confirms with that examiner, finalizes the date and books the room.
